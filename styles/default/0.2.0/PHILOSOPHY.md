# Twin Ink

A visual philosophy for a page that is first of all white. Everything else is ink: one blue, one red, and the grey that both become when they thin. The movement takes its name from the two-colour pencil of the editor and the surveyor, blue for what is drawn, red for what is marked, and from the discipline of letting the paper do most of the work. Nothing is decorated. Whatever appears is either a measured mark or a word that could not be left out, and every one of them is placed as if by a hand that has spent years learning where a mark belongs. The work must look laboured over, the product of countless small corrections by someone at the top of their craft, and it must never look busy.

**Space and form.** White is the material, not the absence of one. Margins are generous and exact, set once and never violated; the safe zone is a real boundary, like the plate edge of an engraving. Forms are thin and continuous: hairlines, curves that resolve into their asymptotes, small filled circles where a value was actually observed. Rectangles are rare and quiet. The page breathes in the proportions of a well-set folio, with the same patient optical correction a master typesetter gives to a column: nothing centred by arithmetic that looks wrong to the eye.

**Colour and material.** Two inks only. Blue is the signal: the thing being shown, the curve, the primary series, the quantity under study. Red is attention: the comparison, the highlighted term, the mark that says look here. Each ink has a full value and a soft tint, and the tints are made as if by diluting the ink on paper, never by lightening with grey. Text is a blue-black that reads as ink rather than as pixels. Secondary text and axes recede through a cool grey that belongs to the same family as the paper. No third hue is ever introduced; emphasis is achieved by weight, by tint and by distance, which is how it was achieved for four hundred years before colour was cheap.

**Scale and rhythm.** Rhythm comes from repetition of a measured mark: families of curves spaced by a constant parameter, ticks at constant intervals, dots at the integer positions where something was recorded. Dense accumulation is welcome when it is systematic; a reader who looks for a long time should keep finding structure. Scale is hierarchical but gentle: one large quiet gesture, a middle register of labelled structure, and a whisper of reference marks. Letters are set in a plain grotesque with even colour, tracked slightly open at small sizes and left unforced at large ones. Mathematics is set in a humanist face with real italics and a true fraction bar, because a formula is a sentence and deserves a typeface that was drawn to be read.

**Composition and balance.** Compositions resolve around an asymmetric centre of gravity, with the primary structure pulled toward one side and the margin on the other left wide for marginalia: a small formula, a reference ladder, a plate number. Alignment is to a baseline grid that is never shown and always felt. Every text element sits clear of every graphic; nothing touches, nothing crosses, nothing is clipped. The result should feel like a plate from a treatise of an invisible discipline, where something ephemeral has been observed carefully enough to be drawn with confidence.

**Hierarchy and restraint.** Words are labels, not explanation. The largest type on a page is still modest; the smallest is still legible at arm's length when the page is printed or at presentation size when it is projected. Ink is spent where meaning is, and nowhere else. The craft is in what was removed: a draft with half the elements and twice the precision is always the better draft. The finished page must look inevitable, as though it could not have been arranged any other way, and as though it took as long to make as it will take to understand.
